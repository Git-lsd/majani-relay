# Prior art: who else does this, and what is left that is ours

Searched on the evening of 3 October 2026 (US Eastern time). Every item below has a link. The "Check" mark says how we know:

- **Read**: we opened the page, app-store listing, abstract, dataset record or README ourselves on 3 Oct 2026. "Read (summary)" means a fetch tool returned a summary of the page, which can drop details.
- **Snippet**: we saw it only in a search-result summary. Open the source before quoting it.

"Not found" always means "not found in our searches". It is not proof that nothing exists.

## 1. Summary

Coffee leaf photo apps already exist, including in East Africa: PlantVillage Nuru has had a coffee rust model in Uganda since 2022, KawaScan works offline in Uganda, a University of Rwanda app sends GPS-tagged coffee leaf reports to agronomists, and Digital Green's FarmerChat takes photos from Kenyan coffee farmers at scale. Coffee rust surveillance also exists: Central America, Mexico and Colombia have run standard plot counts with phone forms and regional alerts since 2013–2014, without photos or image AI. Other parts of our design are not new either. Saying "not sure" is published: FarmerChat rejects poor photos and asks for another, and a coffee paper rejects predictions below a confidence threshold (Pham et al. 2025). Counting a standard sample of leaves per plot is the routine method of those surveillance programmes. The lab-to-field drop we measured has been reported before, for coffee on the same Ecuador dataset (Zuñiga Cajas et al. 2025) and for other crops with the same "confidently wrong" pattern (Xiang et al. 2026). What we did not find, for coffee or in any extension tool, is the full loop Majani Relay runs offline: a relay farmer's standard plot photos become rust counts; unclear photos go to a named officer; the officer's labels update the model **on the phone**; and villages are ranked for the officer's next visit, with an adjustment for small samples. Each piece has a precedent somewhere. Our claim is the combination, for coffee co-ops, and we say "not found", never "first".

**Which of our models the numbers describe.** Every number about our own models is generated from `results/metrics.json` and `results/uganda_external.json` (filled into this page from `results/numbers.json`; details in `results/RESULTS.md`). The repository has two models:
- **Shipped model** (head v3-2026-10-03-lab+field+other, key `shipped`): trained on lab photos plus RoCoLe field photos (Ecuador, robusta), with an "other problem" answer learned from red spider mite photos (counts in `results/RESULTS.md`, section 2). On field photos it did not train on (5-fold cross-validation) it answers 91%, right on 89% of those (section 3.1). On 60 RoCoLe photos kept out from the start it answers 50, 88% right (section 3.4). On Ugandan farm photos it never trained on, it sends 93% to the officer; forced, it would be right on 43% (section 3.3). Our RoCoLe copy has no plant IDs, so leaves of one plant can sit in both training and test folds; the cross-validated numbers may be too high.
- **Lab-only model** (key `lab_only_new_region_simulation`): trained on lab photos only. It gave the finding that shaped the design (2.5% right on field photos at 93% average confidence), and we use it to simulate a region whose photos the model has never seen ("not sure" to 99.6% of field photos; after 50 officer labels it answers 93%, 82% right; `results/RESULTS.md`, section 3.6).

## 3. Our differentiators: which hold

We tested six possible differentiators against the search. Three hold as "not found" results. We say "not found", never "first" or "unique".

### What holds, and how we word it

1. **The combination.**
   > "For coffee, we found no tool that turns a relay farmer's standard plot photos into rust counts, says 'not sure', queues unclear photos for the officer and gives a village visit order. Each part exists elsewhere: FAO FAMEWS (maize) and CottonAce (cotton) turn field checks into counts and area decisions, and SATCAFE, Mexico and Guatemala do it for coffee with counts by eye."

2. **Learning from the officer's labels on the phone.**
   > "Research has shown phone apps that learn new diseases from an agronomist's photos (Petrellis 2019, 2022) and training on the device for plant disease (ToEFL 2024). We found no coffee or extension tool where the officer's labels on 'not sure' photos update the model on the phone."

   Evidence: on Ugandan farm photos the shipped model never trained on, the on-phone update (exactly as in the app) raises "right if forced" from 43% to 81% after 50 labels and 85% after 100, and, on Ugandan photos never used to choose any setting, the app answers 46% of photos after 100 labels with 94% of those answers correct, against 7% and 53% with no labels (`results/RESULTS.md`, section 3.3). The Ugandan folder labels stand in for an officer. Part of that comes from a second familiarity check (a photo close to one officer-labelled photo counts as familiar); its cutoff was chosen on a separate part of the Ugandan photos. In the Ecuador simulation with the lab-only model, 50 labels let it answer 93% of photos, 82% right (section 3.6).
   - Petrellis 2019, citrus, a phone app with no server where "the end user (e.g., an agronomist)" defines disease signatures from a few photos: https://doi.org/10.3390/app9091952 (Read: abstract)
   - Petrellis et al. 2022, tomato and pear, agronomists train the classifier with 40–100 photos per disease: https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy12081732 (Read: abstract)
   - ToEFL (Mitra, Mohanty, Kougianos 2024), training on the edge device for plant disease detection, "suitable for a remote village where internet service may be poor": https://doi.org/10.1145/3649476.3660381 (Read: abstract)
   - Closest in coffee: an IEEE paper on active learning for coffee leaves picks images for an expert to label, on a server (https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/11162523/, Snippet).

3. **Village ranking with a small-sample adjustment.**
   > "Small-sample adjustment is a standard method in disease mapping. We found no coffee or co-op tool that uses it to rank villages for officer visits. Our test uses synthetic villages."

   Evidence: in our simulation at the shipped model's error rates, the adjustment cuts false alarms from 3.4 to 1.0 per 40 villages but misses more outbreaks near the alert line (1.6 to 3.0; `results/RESULTS.md`, section 3.5). It ranks rust already seen; it does not predict outbreaks.
   - The method: Clayton & Kaldor 1987, empirical Bayes for disease maps: https://doi.org/10.2307/2532003 (title and date only)
   - Kenya coffee rust: Wanyonyi et al. 2026 use county random effects (a form of pooling) to **predict** rust from weather and plot data, on 9,850 plot observations from KALRO-CRI in six counties. They do not rank areas: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pclm.0000754 (PLOS Climate, 21 Apr 2026, Read: abstract and article page)
   - Survey design: the CATIE/CIRAD guide (2019) handles small samples when planning how many plots to visit, not when reporting: https://agritrop.cirad.fr/595182/1/Gu%C3%ADa%20vigilancia%20-%20VF.pdf (Read)
   - Other uses of small-area estimation in pest monitoring: Nur et al. 2026, planthoppers, Indonesia: https://doi.org/10.22146/jpti.108155 (Read: abstract)

### Not differentiators on their own (supporting facts)

- **Saying "not sure" on its own.** FarmerChat rejects poor photos and asks for retakes. Several apps show a confidence. Among coffee papers, Pham et al. 2025 reject predictions below per-class confidence thresholds (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-95849-3, Read). The method behind our familiarity check (distance to the nearest stored photos) is published: Sun et al. 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06507 (Read (summary)). What holds: "Among the published coffee papers we found, none tests rejecting coffee field photos that are unlike the training photos." Our tests: for the lab-only model, the familiarity check separates Ecuador field photos from lab photos with AUROC 0.999 (an easy shift; section 3.6); for the shipped model, it sends 93% of Ugandan farm photos to the officer, where forced answers would be right only 43% of the time (section 3.3). These are not comparable with other studies' numbers.
- **Finding the lab-to-field gap.** What holds: "Our numbers confirm, for coffee, the lab-to-field drop that others report." Sources:
  - Xiang, Shi, Zhu 2026: a model trained on lab photos (PlantVillage) fell 67.7 points in accuracy on field photos (PlantDoc), while its mean confidence stayed at 79.76%: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2026.1826962 (Read: abstract)
  - Zuñiga Cajas et al. 2025, coffee rust severity: F1 about 0.75–0.80 on the training source, 0.24–0.52 on RoCoLe with no retraining: https://doi.org/10.4114/intartif.vol28iss76pp200-222 (Read: full PDF)
  - Ramcharan et al. 2018, cassava: on real field images with clear symptoms, F1 was 32% lower than on the research test set: https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.08692 (Read: abstract)
  - Ours (lab-only model, forced to answer): 2.5% of RoCoLe field photos right, at 93% average confidence; it called almost every field leaf "Phoma" (`results/RESULTS.md`, section 3.6; `results/metrics.json`, key `lab_only_new_region_simulation.field`). This is why the shipped model trains on field photos.
- **Multi-leaf checks.** Nuru's coffee protocol scans 3 leaves; the surveillance programmes in section 2 count many leaves per plot (Mexico: 20 plants × 10 leaves).
- **Offline, no account, data on the phone, hashed member number.** Offline diagnosis is common (Nuru, KawaScan; KafeCam and the Rwanda app also claim it). A phone app with no server is published (Petrellis 2019). A hashed ID is routine privacy practice.
- **"Stricter evaluation".** Others test across datasets (Zuñiga Cajas 2025 test coffee rust models on RoCoLe with no retraining; Xiang et al. 2026 test a lab-trained model on field photos). Nuru's cassava study compared the app with 60 people against expert labels (Mrisho et al. 2020, https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2020.590889, Read (summary)); our human baseline is 2 team members with a picture card. For the shipped model, RoCoLe is no longer a sealed test, because the model trains on it; its external test is the Ugandan set, which it never trained on.

## 4. Where others are stronger than us

- **Real users and real field use.** Plantix has 10M+ downloads; FarmerChat has 8,805 active users in Kenya with coffee as 12.63% of queries; Nuru's coffee model was introduced to about 1,000 Ugandan farmers. Majani Relay has no field use.
- **Field photos from East Africa.** Nuru's coffee model was built with photos collected in Uganda, and the Rwanda app with photos from Rwanda. Our field training photos are Ecuadorian robusta; we test on Ugandan farm photos but do not train on them. Next step: officer-labelled Kenyan pilot photos (`PILOT_PLAN.md`).
- **Plot sample size.** The CATIE/CIRAD guide recommends 15–30 plants with 2–4 branches per plot; Mexico samples 20 plants × 10 leaves. Our 15 leaves (3 on each of 5 trees) give a less precise plot estimate than these protocols. Next step: the pilot checks how much plot estimates vary, and the number of leaves per plot is raised if they vary too much (`PILOT_PLAN.md`).
- **Pooling many plots into area alerts.** SATCAFE, Mexico, Colombia and Guatemala pool many plots into maps and bulletins, and FAMEWS pools scouting data into country maps. Majani Relay keeps records on each phone, with no server; the co-op combines the phones' exports, and pooling across a region is a roadmap item.
- **Reach on basic phones.** India's Coffee Krishi Taranga sends voice advisories to about 150,000 registered growers (https://precisiondev.org/project/coffee-krishi-taranga/, Read). We need a smartphone with the relay farmer.
- **Problems outside our list.** Plantix covers 780+ problems across 30 crops; our model knows five coffee leaf classes plus an "other problem" answer learned from one pest. That answer sends 38% of red spider mite photos to the officer (our earlier model without it: 14%), but 85 of 167 mite photos are still answered "rust" (`results/RESULTS.md`, sections 3.2 and 3.8). In Uganda, 9% of Phoma leaves are answered "rust" (section 3.3). Next steps (section 3.8): retrain "other" on the officer's "Different problem" photos in the pilot, and train on Ugandan Phoma field photos before the pilot.
- **Forecasting.** CABI PRISE (berry borer), Wanyonyi et al. 2026 (rust) and Embrapa's SAFCAFE forecast risk from weather. We only count what is seen. These tools could complement ours.
- **Berries.** Nuru's coffee model also checks berries (red blister). We see leaves only, so we miss coffee berry disease.
- **Languages.** FAMEWS runs in 31 languages and FarmerChat takes voice. Our Swahili text and audio are cross-checked by machine and against published Swahili farm material; a Swahili-speaking extension officer reviews all 24 answers in pilot week 1 ([LANGUAGE.md](LANGUAGE.md)).

**Why not train on field photos?** We now do, in part, and the trade-off is stated here.
- No open dataset has field photos of Kenyan arabica leaves on the plant. The Kenyan sets (JMuBEN) are cropped close-ups.
- The shipped model trains on most of the RoCoLe field photos (Ecuador, robusta; counts in `results/RESULTS.md`, section 2). The cost: RoCoLe is no longer a sealed test for it. The only photos it never saw are the picture-card, demo and mite photos and their copies, all from RoCoLe, and our RoCoLe copy has no plant IDs.
- A study with 550 Saudi field photos alone got 68.66% on a 4-class disease task, and 97.76% after adding JMuBEN; the authors say the mixed number is not a field estimate (AlFrhan & Almaleh 2026, https://doi.org/10.3390/app16126109, Read: abstract). Small field sets alone go only so far.
- The Uganda set is now our external field test. Next step: add DECAFIA v2 (Colombian part) as a third test that neither model has seen. Who: ML lead. When: after the challenge. The officer-label loop exists because a real region's photos will differ from all of these.

**The three most useful open field datasets for us:** DECAFIA v2 (Colombian images only), the Uganda Soroti set, and BRACOT.
